# **Memory takes years. Access takes weeks.**

### Why a plan that looks balanced on paper still fails.

Most annual plans I see look balanced. Each part of the business has something, the budget is sensibly spread, and the targets add up. And a surprising number of them fail the same way, for a reason not visible on the page. The work in them runs on different clocks, and the plan treats them all as if they run on the same one.

## **Five assets, five speeds**

The five assets don't move at the same pace, and the differences can be large.

Access is the fastest. You can fix a broken checkout in a fortnight. You can add a new stockist in a month. A quoting process that takes three weeks can be brought down to three days by next quarter, if somebody decides it matters enough to do so. The results show up almost immediately.

Value moves more slowly. Changing the product, the proposition, or the way it is priced takes months, and it takes even more months before customers notice and potentially revise their judgement.

Preference sits in between. By preference, I mean the durable tilt toward choosing you rather than an immediate response to an offer; it shifts as people encounter what you have changed and as competitors do things of their own.

Memory is the slowest of all. Getting into the heads of the people who might buy from you, and staying there, is the work of years. Binet and Field’s analysis of effectiveness data found that brand-building effects build over much longer periods than activation effects, which is exactly what you would expect if memory is what is being built. The upside is that memory, once built, also fades slowly. The downside is that usually nothing you do this quarter will show up in it this quarter.

Experience is the odd one out because it doesn't have one speed. It has two. It deteriorates quickly, and it repairs slowly. A cost programme can make a service noticeably worse in weeks. Winning back the trust of customers who noticed can take years, because people remember a bad experience far longer than an adequate one. Once trust is lost, returning to the same business can feel like taking a risk people would rather avoid.

## **The fast work always wins**

Put those speeds into a planning process and something predictable happens. Fast work produces results within the planning period. The slow work does not. So, every time there is pressure on the budget, and let’s be honest, for most businesses there always is, the slow work is the obvious thing to defer. It will not be missed this year. It will be missed in three years, by which time nobody will connect the two.

The following year, the same thing happens, because the slow work still has not started and still will not pay back within the period. I have seen businesses defer the same piece of memory-building work for five years in a row, each time for a good reason, and then wonder why their paid acquisition keeps getting more expensive, or why they have to discount more to win sales they used to charge full price for.

That is the first way a balanced plan fails. The slow work never starts, because the fast work always looks more urgent.

## **Judging slow work on a fast clock**

The second way is more subtle. The slow work starts, then gets reviewed on the fast clock.

A business commits to building memory, runs a year of consistent work, and reviews it at six months against the same short-term measures it uses for everything else. The results are unremarkable, because six months is not long enough for memory to have moved much. The programme is judged to have underperformed; the budget is moved back into things that show results, and the business ends up having paid for the first six months of something without ever getting the benefit.

Measuring slow work on a fast clock is not neutral. It systematically kills the work that would have mattered most. Once that happens, it is remarkably hard to get back onto the plan.

## **Running two clocks**

The answer is not complicated, though it does take some nerve. A plan needs to run two clocks, and to say so explicitly.

Start the slow work first and protect it. Because memory and value take the longest to pay back, they have the most to lose from delay. Name the budget for them as an investment with its own time horizon and measures, so it doesn't compete on equal terms with activity that pays back in weeks.

Use the fast work to release what already exists. Access is where quick wins live, and quick wins buy the credibility and the cash to keep the slow work going. Fixing the leaks that are losing demand you have already created is almost always the best use of the first ninety days.

When it comes to experience, I always say you should watch it carefully because of its asymmetry. Any decision that might make it worse deserves more scrutiny than its potential size suggests, because the damage it creates can be fast and the time to repair it a hell of a sight slower.

And for the slow assets, agree on leading indicators before you start. You cannot wait three years to find out whether memory-building worked. You can track whether more of the right people recognise you, whether you come to mind in more situations where people buy, and agree in advance what movement you would expect to see and by when.
